Part 1 · the funders

Two NIH notices, one NSF stance, and an honest gap 9 min live

Funder rules come first because they carry the heaviest consequences - misconduct territory, not desk rejection. The two NIH notices are the clearest-written AI policies in academia, and they bind different people: one binds reviewers, the other binds applicants. Confusing the two is the most common policy error we see, so the diagram keeps them on separate rails.

LiveReading the NIH pair like a leader5 min▶
  • NOT-OD-23-149 (2023) is about other people's ideas. Generative AI is prohibited in NIH peer review because pasting an application into a tool discloses it - full stop. As a lab head you sit on both sides of this rail: your own proposals are protected by it, and every review you or your senior people write is bound by it. a5 turns this into your lab's review rules.
  • NOT-OD-25-132 (2025) is about your ideas being yours. Applications substantially developed by AI will not be treated as the applicant's original ideas. AI may assist limited aspects - the aims, rationale, design and claims must remain the team's intellectual work. Two teeth worth quoting exactly: a cap of six applications per applicant per year, and post-award discovery of substantial AI development can escalate toward research misconduct. Post-award means this risk never expires at submission.
  • NSF puts it on the proposer. You are responsible for the accuracy and authenticity of your submission, including anything AI-assisted. The PAPPG is revised on a cycle - verify the current version for the year you submit, and say so in your lab's table.

Grants get their own working session in a4; today's job is only to know which notice binds whom.

Self-studyWhy the honest gap row matters more than it looks3 min read▶

The white row in the diagram - ERC, bioRxiv, and every funder this course did not verify - is doing deliberate work. A policy course that asserts confidently about everything is exactly as trustworthy as a chatbot that never says "I do not know". This course's contract is that every claim traces to a primary source checked on a stated date; where that check did not happen, the page says "check the live policy" instead of guessing. Steal the pattern for your lab: a rules table with an honest "unverified" row and a check date beats a confident table nobody can defend. Part 2 · publishers + bodies

The publisher matrix, one screen 9 min live

Journal policies look like a jungle until you see the two patterns: everyone agrees on authorship (no AI authors, anywhere, because authorship carries accountability and a model cannot be accountable), and everyone draws the disclosure line at roughly the same altitude - generation needs declaring, polish mostly does not. The matrix below compresses the six rule-setters your lab most likely meets; the cards carry the exact wording that matters.

LiveThe disclosure line, in the publishers' own terms4 min▶
  • Springer Nature: AI-assisted copy editing of your own text needs no declaration. That is the polish end of the line, stated explicitly.
  • Elsevier: a disclosure statement is required for generative use beyond basic grammar and spelling checks; no AI-generated or AI-altered images; and reviewers must not upload manuscripts to AI tools. Three rules, one publisher - note how the image rule would have caught the Frontiers rat figure from a1 at the policy level.
  • Science: historically the strictest of the major venues on generative AI. If your lab targets it, read its current policy rather than assuming the Springer Nature norms travel.
  • The working summary for your lab: if AI produced sentences or images you kept, disclose; if it fixed your grammar, you are in polish territory. When a case sits on the line, disclose - a one-line statement costs nothing.
LiveICMJE, COPE, arXiv: the bodies that set everyone else's defaults4 min▶
  • ICMJE (the recommendations most medical journals adopt wholesale): describe AI use in the cover letter AND in the submitted work itself - two places, not one. Reviewers must ask the journal's permission before any AI use and disclose it in the review. If your field publishes in ICMJE-following journals, this is effectively your law.
  • COPE: AI cannot be an author because it cannot take responsibility; accountability for the whole work sits with the humans. COPE also expects disclosure of AI use in peer review - and its case guidance is where editors turn when they suspect an AI-written review, which makes it worth a leader's ten minutes.
  • arXiv is the instructive outlier: LLM use is not prohibited, and authors take full responsibility for all contents however generated. The CS section adds teeth - unchecked AI content such as hallucinated citations or residual prompts can draw penalties up to a one-year ban. Permissive on use, ruthless on ownership: the same diagnosis a1 reached from the failure catalogue, arrived at independently by a moderation team.
Self-studyWhy the consensus landed on accountability3 min read▶

It is worth noticing how unusual this is: publishers who agree on almost nothing - open access, embargoes, data mandates - converged within months on "no AI authorship", and for the same stated reason. Authorship is not a byline; it is a liability signature. An author can be asked to produce data, defend a method, correct the record, or face a misconduct panel. A model can do none of those things, so putting one on a byline would create work with no accountable owner - which is precisely the failure mode behind every case in a1's catalogue. The consensus is not really about AI at all; it is the accountability definition of authorship, restated under pressure. That is also why it is the one row of the matrix you never need to re-verify.

Part 3 · the operating system

Running the rails in a real lab 5 min live

A pile of policies is not a policy. Two moves turn the pile into something your group can run without you in the room: a principle that resolves every apparent conflict, and a ritual that keeps the whole thing true.

LiveThe principle: strictest applicable rule, per artifact3 min▶

Policies do not conflict by contradiction; they conflict by strictness, and they attach to artifacts. A proposal is governed by the funder's notice, a manuscript by the target journal's policy, a review by the venue's reviewer rules - and everything by your university's guidance, which in a misconduct proceeding is the document that counts. Within one artifact, when rules overlap, the strictest applicable one is the operative default.

Worked example: your student drafts a manuscript with disclosed AI assistance (fine at the target journal), then recycles two paragraphs into your NIH renewal. The artifact changed, so the rail changed - NOT-OD-25-132 now governs, and "substantially developed by AI" is the test, not the journal's disclosure line. The principle catches this automatically; vibes do not.

LiveThe ritual: quarterly re-verification, fifteen minutes2 min▶

Every policy named on this page has moved at least once since 2023, and this page itself dates its claims (verified August 2026). So the lab table you build in the demo gets a fourth column - "last checked" - and a recurring calendar slot: once a quarter, one person opens each live policy page, confirms or updates the row, and refreshes the date. Fifteen minutes, rotated among senior members so the knowledge does not live in one head.

Assign the rows, not the job "Someone should keep an eye on policies" produces nobody doing it. "Priya owns the NIH rows, Marco owns the Elsevier and ICMJE rows, dates refreshed by the first Friday of the quarter" produces a table you can trust. Named ownership of specific rows - the same medicine this course prescribes for text, applied to rules.
Demo 1 of 1

Your rules-that-bind-us table, built live ★ 13 min · everyone builds

The leader-altitude version of the researcher track's b1 policy check: not "which rules bind me" but "which rules bind my lab" - one table, one page, real sources only. Bring your laptop; leave with the skeleton filled for your actual funder and top journals.

Open a blank doc titled "Rules that bind us - [lab name] - checked [today's date]". Four columns: rule-setter, what it prohibits, what it requires disclosed, last checked.

Row 1 - your funder. NIH labs: NOT-OD-23-149 and NOT-OD-25-132, from the live notices. NSF labs: the current PAPPG. Others (ERC included): find the live page - this course verified neither ERC nor bioRxiv, so your own check is the source of truth here.

Rows 2-3 - your lab's top two target journals. Pull the publisher's generative-AI policy page and record three things: authorship (always no), the disclosure line and where the statement goes, and the reviewer rule.

Row 4 - your university's research-office AI guidance. It may be stricter than everything above, and it is the document a misconduct panel reads first.

Bottom line: write the strictest-applicable-rule-per-artifact sentence in your own words, assign row owners, and put the quarterly check in the calendar before you close the laptop. Unassigned tables die by Christmas.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · The two NIH notices are easily confused. Who does each one bind?

Separate rails, separate people. The 2023 notice protects unpublished ideas from leaking through a reviewer's prompt; the 2025 notice protects the originality of the ideas NIH funds, adds the six-application-per-year cap, and keeps the risk alive post-award. A lab head sits on both rails at once - protected by one, bound by both.

2 · Where does the publisher consensus draw the disclosure line?

The line sits at roughly the same altitude across publishers: if AI produced content you kept, disclose; if it fixed your grammar, you are in polish territory. Elsevier adds no-AI-images and no reviewer uploads; ICMJE wants the disclosure in two places. When a case sits on the line, disclose - the one-line statement is free, the discovery is not.

3 · Your target journal permits disclosed AI drafting, so your lab adopts that as its blanket rule - proposals included. What went wrong?

This is the exact failure the strictest-applicable-rule-per-artifact principle exists to catch. Text that is compliant in a manuscript can put a renewal into "not your original ideas" territory the moment it moves artifacts. Manuscripts follow the journal, proposals follow the funder, reviews follow the venue, everything follows your university - per artifact, strictest wins.
