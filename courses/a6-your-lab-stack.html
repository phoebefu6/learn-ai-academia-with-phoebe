Part 1 · the data question

The data question decides the stack 7 min live

Most lab AI conversations start with "which tool?" and go wrong immediately. The right first question is "which data?" - because the sensitivity of what goes into the prompt determines which tools are even eligible. Three tiers cover everything a lab handles.

LiveConsumer terms vs enterprise guarantees4 min▶

The tier model turns a values debate into a reading exercise. A consumer chat tool is a product with data-retention terms - some retain prompts, some train on them under some settings, and the terms change. Nobody in your lab should be guessing: the rule is that tier 2 material only enters tools whose current terms someone has actually read, with the date of reading recorded.

Enterprise instances are the genuine exception. If your institution runs an approved deployment with contractual no-training and retention guarantees, tier 2 work opens up substantially - drafts, unpublished results, internal documents. The leadership move costs one email: ask IT or the research office three questions. Do we have an enterprise AI instance? What are its data guarantees? Who approved it for research use? Most PIs have never asked, and the answer changes what half the researcher track's sessions can do with real material.

LiveTier 3 is not yours to relax3 min▶

Participant data sits under ethics approvals that were written before this tool generation and remain binding. Collaborators' drafts and manuscripts you review sit under confidentiality you accepted (a5 covered the review case in full). Neither is yours to trade for convenience, and no enterprise guarantee automatically clears it either:

  • Human-subjects data: the IRB or ethics office is the authority on whether any AI processing is compatible with the consent and the protocol. This course's honest position is to route you to them, not to rule for them.
  • Third-party material: the default is never, and the exception is explicit permission from the person whose work it is.
  • The de-identification trap: "I removed the names" is not a tier change. Whether a dataset is de-identified to the standard your approval requires is an ethics-office judgement, not a vibe.
Self-studyWhere your students meet these tiers2 min read▶

The researcher track plants the same boundary in b1's ground rules ("never paste what is not yours to share") and leans on it hardest in b4 (extraction from PDFs) and b7 (data analysis). Your policy's data-tier section - drafted in today's demo - is what turns that classroom rule into a lab rule with your name on it.

Part 2 · the stack

Free-first, paid per demonstrated need 7 min live

The stack below runs a whole lab at zero subscription cost, and every line of it is vendor-verified or peer-reviewed - no marketing claims. The principle is free-first, not free-only: paid tools earn their line in the budget when a specific person demonstrates a specific need the free tier cannot meet. Never stack-first.

LiveWhat paid buys, and the funding rule3 min▶

Three paid tools come up most, and each has a legitimate trigger:

  • Elicit Pro - when someone is actually running a systematic-review workflow at scale, not merely searching. The peer-reviewed framing of its extraction is "usable with human verification", which is also the policy line to attach to it.
  • scite - when citation-context checking (which papers support vs contrast a claim) becomes a recurring need rather than a curiosity.
  • Covidence - when a formal systematic review with a team needs managed screening workflow and audit trail.

The funding rule that keeps this honest: a named person, a named project, a demonstrated need the free tier failed to meet - then the subscription. Buying the full stack up front buys shelfware and, worse, signals to the lab that tools rather than verification habits are the point.

Self-studyThe stack review ritual2 min read▶

Put the stack on the same quarterly cycle as the policy (Part 3): who is using what, which subscription earned its cost, which free tier changed its terms. Fifteen minutes in a lab meeting. The point is not tool churn - it is that a stack chosen once and never revisited quietly drifts out of both usefulness and compliance, and the quarterly date is the cheapest insurance available.

Part 3 · the artifact

The one-page lab AI policy 6 min live

Everything this track taught compresses into six short sections that fit on one page. That constraint is the design: a policy your students can reconstruct from memory gets followed; a comprehensive twelve-pager gets filed. Followable beats comprehensive, every time.

LiveWhy one page, argued properly3 min▶

The one-page constraint is not minimalism for its own sake. Three reasons it wins:

  • Recall is the compliance mechanism. Your students make AI decisions at midnight, alone, with no document open. The only policy operating at that moment is the one they can remember - which is why the three ground rules are three, and short.
  • A page can be re-verified quarterly in fifteen minutes. A twelve-pager cannot, so it silently goes stale, and a stale policy is worse than none - it teaches people that the written rules and the real rules differ.
  • A page fits in an onboarding conversation. New lab member, week one, ten minutes: here is what we do and why. That conversation, not the document, is where the norm actually transfers - the a3 supervision session makes the same point about authorship norms.
Self-studyWhat the policy deliberately does not decide2 min read▶

Three boundaries keep the page honest. It does not overrule external rule-holders - funder notices, journal policies and university guidance always win, which is why section 2's middle rule says "the policy in force", not "this policy". It does not rule on human-subjects data - section 4 routes tier 3 questions to the IRB or ethics office, the actual authority. And it does not freeze the tool list - tools live in an appendix or a lab wiki, so the page survives the stack changing under it.

Demo

Draft the policy live, section by section ★ 18 min · every leader drafts

Not a template handout - a working session. Six sections, a few minutes each, your lab's real answers. Leaders leave with a filled skeleton, and the homework turns it into the signed page on the wall.

Section 1 - scope: write one sentence naming who is covered (everyone in the lab, including visiting students and RAs) and what is covered (every research artifact that leaves the lab under its name).

Section 2 - the three ground rules: copy them verbatim, then read them aloud once. Never cite what you have not read. Disclose what the policy in force asks you to disclose. Never paste what is not yours to share. They are the researcher track's contract (b1) - your policy and their training now say the same words.

Section 3 - per-stage allowances: one line per lifecycle stage. Where is AI welcome (search, screening with verification, code with tests, polish per journal rules), where is it conditional, where never (peer review material, final claims). Steal the a1 capability map's shape.

Section 4 - data tiers: copy the three tiers from Part 1, then localise tier 2: does your institution have an enterprise instance? If you do not know, write "UNKNOWN - email sent [date]" and actually send the email today.

Section 5 - disclosure habit: decide where the one-line-per-artifact record lives (lab notebook, repo readme, shared doc) and say explicitly that disclosure carries no stigma - a4 and a3 both showed why the no-stigma clause is what makes disclosure real.

Section 6 - the date: pick the quarterly re-verification day, name its owner, and put it in the calendar before the session ends. A policy without a re-verification date is a snapshot; the date is what makes it a policy.

★ Section 2, as it should read on your page

Ground rules (all members, all artifacts): 1. Never cite what you have not read - every AI-suggested reference is resolved and read before it enters our work. 2. Disclose per the policy in force - funder, journal and university rules win, and the strictest applicable rule is our default. 3. Never paste what is not yours to share - participant data, collaborators' drafts and manuscripts under review do not enter AI tools. Questions about human-subjects data go to the ethics office, not to this page.

If the room stalls on section 3 Do not litigate every stage today. Write the two easy "never" lines (review material, final claims), the two easy "yes" lines (search, code with tests), and mark the rest "decide by [next lab meeting]". A policy that ships with two open items beats a perfect draft that never leaves this room.
Homework

Ship it - this week ◐ 40-50 min total

  • Send the enterprise-instance email if you did not in session: does the institution run an approved AI instance, what are its data guarantees, who approved it for research use. The answer rewrites your tier 2 line.
  • Finish the two open items in section 3, format the page, and put it where the lab actually looks - wiki front page, wet-lab door, repo readme. One page, one location, no PDF graveyard.
  • Run the ten-minute onboarding conversation with your current members, not just future ones - walk the page once, invite pushback, and adjust anything that would not survive contact with real work.
  • Claim the free stack: verified-teacher Copilot via GitHub Education, ASReview and Zotero installed, a NotebookLM trial on a paper you know well - and check each plan page's current terms as you go, since these move.
  • Put the quarterly date in your calendar with a fifteen-minute agenda: policies still current? Terms still as read? Stack still earning its place?

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · A student has "anonymised" interview transcripts and wants to paste them into a consumer chatbot for thematic coding. What does the tier model say?

"I removed the names" is not a tier change - whether de-identification meets the standard the ethics approval requires is an IRB/ethics-office judgement, not the student's or yours. Participant data sits in tier 3, where consumer tools are never eligible and the authority is the ethics office. Option B applies the tier 2 rule to tier 3 material - the exact confusion the written tiers exist to prevent.

2 · Why does the stack start free-first instead of buying the recommended tools up front?

The free layer is not a compromise: open infrastructure (Semantic Scholar, OpenAlex), a peer-review-validated screener (ASReview, Nature Machine Intelligence 2021), Zotero, NotebookLM's free tier, and education-plan Copilot. Paid tools have real triggers - Elicit Pro for systematic-review workflow, scite for citation contexts, Covidence for team reviews - and buying them before the need exists produces shelfware. Option C is false: free consumer tools have retention terms too, which is why the tiers apply to everything.

3 · What makes a lab AI policy one that people actually follow?

The compliance mechanism is recall: AI decisions happen alone, at odd hours, with no document open, so the operating policy is whatever fits in memory. One page keeps it re-verifiable in fifteen minutes a quarter; the dated owner keeps it current; comprehensive twelve-pagers go stale and teach the lab that written rules and real rules differ. Followable beats comprehensive.
