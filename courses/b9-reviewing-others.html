Reviewing others: a manuscript held in trust

At some point soon an editor will hand you someone else's unpublished work and ask for your judgement. That manuscript is not yours - not to keep, not to quote, and above all not to paste into an AI tool, however convenient that would be at 11pm before the deadline. This session covers the strictest AI rules in all of academia, why they exist, how to still review well and efficiently inside them, and what changes when you are on the receiving end and drafting the rebuttal.

Part 0

Where this sits in the track

Every session so far worked on YOUR material: your search, your data, your draft. This one flips the table. When you review, the material belongs to someone else and was given to the journal in confidence - which is why the AI rules for reviewers are the tightest rules this course covers. Learn them before your first invitation, because the first breach is usually committed in good faith by someone who never read the policy.

Part 1 · the rulebook

The rules when the manuscript is not yours 8 min live

Four rule-holders have already spoken, and they agree more than they differ. NIH NOT-OD-23-149 prohibits generative AI in NIH peer review outright - uploading any part of an application is a confidentiality breach. Elsevier and Springer Nature both forbid reviewers from uploading manuscripts to AI tools. ICMJE says ask the journal's permission first, keep confidentiality, and disclose any AI use in the review itself. COPE publishes case guidance for editors handling suspected AI-written reviews. Verify the current version of each before your next review - these pages move - but the shape has been stable: the manuscript stays inside the circle of trust.

Notice what is NOT here: a rule-holder who says "fine if the tool does not train on your data." No policy in this set carves out private modes, enterprise instances or local models for the manuscript itself. The breach is the disclosure beyond the circle of trust, not the vendor's retention setting - though your journal may have its own more detailed stance, so verify the current version before you rely on this summary.

LiveThe scale of the temptation - why the rules got written4 min▶

Two measured numbers explain why every rule-holder moved at once:

  • Reviewing is enormous unpaid labour. Aczel et al. (2021) estimated reviewers worldwide spent over 100 million hours on peer review in 2020. Every one of those hours belongs to someone with a deadline of their own - the pressure to shortcut is structural, not a personal failing.
  • And the shortcut is already being taken. Liang et al. (ICML 2024) estimated 6.5-16.9% of the text in AI-conference peer reviews was likely substantially LLM-modified. The rules are not hypothetical fences; they are a response to something already happening at scale.

Hold both at once: the temptation is legitimate and the line is absolute. This session's Part 2 is about relieving the first without crossing the second.

Self-studyWhat "derived from it" covers - the edge cases people argue3 min read▶

The left lane says "anything derived from them," and that phrase does real work. A paraphrase of the methods section encodes the methods. A bullet summary of the contribution encodes the contribution. "A paper I am reviewing proposes [their idea] - is this novel?" hands the idea itself to the tool. Anonymising the authors does not help, because the confidential thing is the work, not the byline. The clean test from the figure: if the prompt would let a stranger reconstruct anything the authors have not published, it is the manuscript, and it stays in the left lane. Part 2 · the craft

Reviewing well WITH AI, legally 7 min live

The rules leave a real working strip. Nothing about the paper touches the tool - but your own prose, your own structure, and general knowledge that does not encode the paper are usable, subject to the journal's rules and your disclosure. The bigger lever, though, is not AI at all: it is knowing what a good review looks like, because most weak reviews fail the quality bar long before they fail the AI policy.

LiveThe legal working strip, precisely drawn4 min▶

What never touches the tool: the manuscript, the data, the authors' identities, anything derived from them. What may - subject to the journal's rules and disclosed in the review:

  • Polishing your own review prose, after it is written. The judgements, the evidence, the specifics are already on the page; the tool tightens sentences you authored. The moment the tool supplies a criticism you did not have, you are past polish.
  • General-knowledge questions that do not encode the paper. "What are the standard threats to validity in stepped-wedge trials?" is a textbook question. "Does THIS trial handle them?" is your job, answered by reading.
  • Structure checklists on your own text. "Does my review address novelty, methods, evidence and clarity, and does every criticism name a fix?" - the checklist runs over your words, not theirs.

And the ICMJE sequence is strict: permission from the journal FIRST, then the use, then disclosure of the use in the review. A perfect disclosure does not repair a use the journal never approved.

Self-studyWhy "names the fix" is the half that matters3 min read▶

Editors triage reviews by usefulness, and usefulness is almost entirely in the fixes. "The framing is weak" gives an author nothing and an editor nothing; "the contribution claimed in the abstract is broader than the evidence in section 5 - either narrow the claim or add the comparison condition" gives both a decision to make. A useful discipline: for every criticism, write the sentence the authors would need to act on it. If you cannot write that sentence, you have an impression, not yet a finding - go back to the paper. This is also your best defence against the temptation Part 1 measured: a reviewer who works fix-first is doing the one part of the job no tool can do, so there is nothing left to outsource illegally.

Part 3 · the other chair

The author's side of the table 5 min live

Sooner than you review, you will be reviewed - and the response-to-reviewers document has its own version of every rule in this session. The good news: your rebuttal is YOUR text about YOUR paper, so it lives under the manuscript rules you already know from b8. We searched for rebuttal-specific publisher rules and found none - a verified absence, not an oversight - so the manuscript policies govern by default. Verify the current version of your journal's policy before submitting, as always.

LiveWhat holds and what changes in the rebuttal4 min▶
  • Organizing your response with AI assistance on your own text is manuscript-rules territory. Structuring the point-by-point, tightening your replies, checking tone - the same disclosure logic as b8 applies, under whatever policy your journal runs.
  • But reviewer comments are held in trust too - by you, now. Reviews often quote your unpublished results, describe your unreleased methods, or reveal the reviewer's own unpublished thinking. Pasting reviewer comments containing unpublished detail into a consumer tool leaks confidential material exactly the way a reviewer uploading your manuscript would. Same principle, other chair.
  • The point-by-point discipline: every comment gets a numbered reply; every reply says what changed and where, or why nothing changed; no comment is silently skipped. Editors read the response before they re-read the paper - the response IS the case for acceptance.
  • Disagreeing is allowed - with evidence and respect. "We respectfully disagree: the reviewer's concern assumes X, but our design controls for it in section 3.2, and [author-year] reports the same pattern" beats capitulating to a change you believe is wrong. Reviewers are wrong sometimes; the rebuttal is where you say so, on the record, with receipts.
Self-studyA response skeleton that survives hostile reading3 min read▶

One structure covers nearly every rebuttal: (1) a short cover note thanking reviewers and summarising the major changes in three or four lines; (2) the point-by-point, with each reviewer comment quoted or faithfully paraphrased, numbered R1.1, R1.2, R2.1...; (3) each reply in three moves - acknowledge what is right in the comment, state the change with its exact location, or state the disagreement with its evidence. Write the hardest reply first while your patience is fresh. And if AI helped you organize or polish the response, apply your journal's manuscript disclosure rules to the response too - it is a submitted document like any other.

Demo 1 of 2

The confidentiality drill ★ 12 min · everyone sorts

A fictional setup: you are reviewing "Manuscript 4471" for a mid-tier journal in your field - a study with unpublished data, a novel method, and a two-week deadline you are behind on. Sort the ten actions below into never / with permission / fine. Then argue the borderline ones out loud - the arguing is the exercise.

Sort these ten, alone, in two minutes: (1) upload the PDF to a chatbot for a summary; (2) paste the abstract to "simplify it"; (3) ask a general tool "what are common pitfalls in diary-study designs?"; (4) paste your own finished review draft for a grammar pass; (5) paste the methods section, authors removed, to check the statistics; (6) email the editor asking what the journal's AI policy permits; (7) run a structure checklist over your own review text; (8) describe the paper's novel method from memory and ask "is this new?"; (9) look up published papers the manuscript cites; (10) ask a tool to draft the review from your bullet points about the paper.

Compare with a neighbour. Expected splits: never - 1, 2, 5, 8, 10 (all encode the manuscript or its content; anonymising in 5 changes nothing; in 10 your bullets ARE the paper's content). With permission + disclosure - 4 and 7 (your own text only). Fine - 3, 6, 9 (general knowledge, asking, and published sources).

Argue the two borderline ones. Action 3 is fine only while the question stays generic - the moment you add the paper's population, measures or twist, it becomes action 8. Action 10 feels like "my own notes" but the notes derive from the manuscript, and derived content is the manuscript.

Write your one-line rule from the drill: "before any prompt during a review, I ask - who else sees the authors' work if I send this?"

The 11pm test Every action in the never lane gets more tempting as the deadline gets closer, and the rationalisations arrive on schedule: it is anonymised, it is a private mode, it is just a summary. Decide the lanes now, while nothing is due. Reviewers who improvise the ethics under time pressure are how the Liang et al. (2024) numbers happened.
Demo 2 of 2

Your review, against the quality bar ★ 10 min · everyone builds

Take a review you have written - or spend the first five minutes drafting one for a preprint in your field, which is public and therefore safe practice material. Then run the bar over it, criticism by criticism.

List every criticism in your review as one line each. Most reviews turn out to contain fewer distinct criticisms than their word count suggests - that is finding number one.

Mark each line S / A / E: is it Specific (names the section and the problem), Actionable (names the fix), Evidence-cited (anchors the objection in something checkable)? A line with none of the three is an impression wearing a review's clothes.

Rewrite your weakest line to clear all three. This is human work on the paper - notice that no tool could have done it, because it requires knowing what the manuscript actually says.

Only now, polish: if your journal permits it and you will disclose it, run YOUR finished text through a grammar pass. Note the disclosure line you would attach: "Language of this review was polished with [tool]; all assessments and evidence are the reviewer's own."

★ The only review prompt that is safely yours - structure, on your text, nothing of theirs

Here is the full text of a peer review I have written. Do not add, remove or soften any criticism. Check only: (1) does every criticism name a specific location and a concrete fix; (2) is the summary paragraph consistent with the severity of the points below it; (3) is the tone professional throughout? List any line that fails, quoting my words back to me. The review text follows: [paste YOUR review - and only after your journal has permitted AI use]

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · A reviewer uploads a manuscript to a private AI mode that "does not train on your data." Under the rules in Part 1, where does this land?

NIH NOT-OD-23-149 treats the upload itself as the confidentiality breach, and Elsevier and Springer Nature ban reviewers from uploading manuscripts without a retention-setting exception. The confidential thing is the work, not the byline - anonymising changes nothing. Held in trust means it goes nowhere, whatever the vendor's settings say.

2 · Your journal has no visible AI policy and you want a grammar pass on your finished review. What does ICMJE say the order of operations is?

ICMJE is permission-first: ask before any AI use in reviewing, and disclose the use in the review itself. A perfect disclosure does not repair a use the journal never approved, and an absent policy is a reason to ask the editor - which is always in the fine lane - not a licence to improvise.

3 · You are drafting a response to reviewers and want AI help organizing it. What does held-in-trust mean on the author's side of the table?

No rebuttal-specific publisher rules were found - a verified absence - so your journal's manuscript rules govern your own response text, disclosure included. But reviews often quote your unpublished results or the reviewer's unpublished thinking, so pasting reviewer comments into a consumer tool leaks confidential material exactly the way a reviewer uploading your manuscript would. Same principle, other chair.
