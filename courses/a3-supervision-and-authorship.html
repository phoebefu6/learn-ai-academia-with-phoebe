Supervision and authorship: norms your group can actually follow

Your students and postdocs already use AI - the only question is whether they use it under norms you wrote together or norms that wrote themselves in the dark. This session gives you the two conversations a PI cannot delegate: what authorship and disclosure rules actually say (they are clearer than the anxiety suggests), and how to set group norms that people follow because following them is easier than hiding. Detection tools are not on the menu, and Part 1 shows you why.

Part 1 · the baseline

Start from reality, not from the policy binder 8 min live

The starting condition of every supervision conversation: AI assistance is already inside your group's workflow - in drafts, in code, in literature searches - whether or not anyone has said so out loud. A policy vacuum does not keep AI out; it just keeps the usage invisible, unexamined and unverifiable. The goal of this session is followable norms, not performative bans.

LiveWhy a ban you cannot enforce is worse than no rule at all4 min▶

A rule that everyone quietly breaks teaches your group exactly one thing: that your rules are decoration. The failure sequence is predictable:

  • The ban goes up. Usually after a scare - a retraction in the news, a colleague's horror story.
  • Usage continues, but goes silent. The tools are in every browser and every reference manager. Students stop asking questions about them, which means they stop learning to use them safely.
  • The first real problem surfaces late. A fabricated citation, an uploaded draft, an AI-shaped paragraph in a submission - discovered by a reviewer or an editor instead of by you, because your group had no channel for catching it early.

The alternative is not permissiveness. It is precision: name what is allowed with conditions, name what is never allowed, and make the disclosure path cheaper than the hiding path. Parts 2 and 3 build exactly that.

LiveWhy detection is the wrong tool for supervision4 min▶

The tempting move - run student text through an AI detector - fails on two counts, one practical and one ethical:

  • Detectors are unreliable in both directions. They miss AI text that has been lightly edited, and they flag human text that happens to be plain and formulaic. A score is not evidence; it is a guess wearing a percentage.
  • The errors are not evenly distributed. Liang et al. (Patterns 2023) found that GPT detectors are biased against non-native English writers - human-written essays by non-native speakers were disproportionately flagged as AI-generated. The widely quoted exact percentage is unverified in this course's source map - check the live paper before quoting a number. The bias finding itself stands, and its supervision implication is brutal: a detector-led lab policy systematically points suspicion at your international students.

An accusation built on a detector score can end a supervisory relationship and cannot survive an appeal. If your instinct says a draft changed voice overnight, the right tool is a conversation about the work - "walk me through how you verified this section" - not a screenshot of a probability.

Self-studyProvenance beats detection: what Grammarly Authorship actually does3 min read▶

There is a structurally better idea than detection, worth knowing about even if you never mandate it: provenance. Grammarly Authorship records how text entered a document as it is being written - typed by the author, pasted from elsewhere, or generated by AI - and can produce a report of that history. Per the vendor's documentation it is a record-keeping tool, not a detector.

  • Detection asks: "does this finished text look AI-shaped?" - a guess, made after the fact, with the bias problem above.
  • Provenance asks: "how did this text arrive?" - a log, kept during writing, that the author controls and can show voluntarily.

The supervision use is not surveillance - it is giving a student who worked honestly a way to demonstrate it, which flips the incentive from hiding to documenting. If AI-use questions in teaching and coursework are your bigger concern, that side belongs to the AI + Education course; this session stays with research supervision.

Part 2 · authorship

The rules are clear; the behaviour is not 8 min live

On authorship the landscape is unusually unanimous, and your students should hear it from you before a journal teaches it to them the hard way. The disclosure landscape is where reality and rulebook diverge - and where your group can quietly do better than the field.

LiveWhy the no-authorship consensus is universal - and worth explaining, not just citing4 min▶

Springer Nature, Elsevier, Science/AAAS, Wiley, IEEE, ACM - not one grants AI authorship, and the rationale is identical everywhere: authorship carries accountability. An author approves the final version, answers for the work's integrity, and can respond when questions come. A model can do none of those things. It cannot be corrected by a corrigendum, cannot respond to an integrity inquiry, cannot take responsibility for a fabricated reference it produced.

Teach the rationale, not just the rule. A student who only knows "no AI authors" will still be confused about acknowledgments, contribution statements and disclosure. A student who understands "authorship = accountability" can derive every downstream answer: the AI goes wherever instruments and assistance go, and never where responsibility sits.

LiveThe ghost problem: undisclosed use is the norm, not the exception4 min▶

Here is the number that should shape your group norms more than any policy text. An arXiv preprint analysis (2025) of post-2023 papers found that only about 0.1% disclosed AI use - while roughly 70% of journals had an AI policy in place. Read those together: the rules exist, and almost nobody follows the disclosure part.

That gap is not mostly fraud. It is what you get when disclosure feels stigmatising, the rules feel ambiguous, and silence has never once been punished. Rational people choose the ghost path. Which means the fix is local and cultural, not procedural:

  • Make disclosure cheap: a standing sentence template (Demo, section 3) that takes thirty seconds to fill in.
  • Make it stigma-free: the PI discloses first, in their own papers, out loud in group meeting. Nothing else you do will signal more.
  • Make non-disclosure the anomaly: when every manuscript in the group carries an AI-use note (even "none"), omitting one becomes the thing that needs explaining.
Real world

The cost of the ghost path is already documented. The "Certainly, here is a possible introduction" retraction (Elsevier, 2024) was not punished for using AI - it was retracted for undisclosed use that nobody on the author side had even read carefully. Disclosed and verified use survives scrutiny; ghosted use fails it in public.

Self-studyWhere each journal draws the polish line - the 3-minute refresher3 min read▶

From a2, the working summary your students will ask you to repeat: Springer Nature treats AI-assisted copy editing of author-written text as not requiring declaration; Elsevier exempts basic grammar and spelling checks but requires a disclosure statement for generative use in writing; Science has historically been the strictest of the majors. The safe default for a mixed-portfolio group: if the AI produced sentences you kept, disclose; if it fixed your grammar, you are in polish territory - and verify the current version of each policy at submission time, because these pages move.

Part 3 · the conversation

Group norms worth writing down 6 min live

The supervision conversation goes wrong when it is abstract ("use AI responsibly") and goes right when it is concrete: two lanes, written down, with one habit that makes the whole thing auditable. This is the skeleton the demo turns into your group's one-pager.

LiveThe show-your-verification habit: the norm that carries all the others3 min▶

One habit converts the allowed lane from a loophole into a discipline: whenever AI assisted, the student can show what they checked. Not a confession - a working note. "AI suggested these five references; I resolved all five to DOIs, two were mangled, kept three." "AI drafted this function; here are my tests." "AI raised these objections to my design; here is my response to each."

  • It makes supervision conversations concrete: you review verification notes, not vibes.
  • It trains exactly the calibration the researcher track (b1-b10) builds - your group members who take that track will recognise every move.
  • It produces, as a side effect, the disclosure content a journal or funder might later ask for. The record already exists.
Self-studyHandling the awkward cases before they arrive3 min read▶
  • The student who over-relies: the tell is not AI-shaped prose, it is a verification trail that is thin or absent. Respond to the missing trail, not the style - "walk me through how you checked this" is recoverable; "I think a machine wrote this" is not.
  • The student who is afraid to use anything: also a supervision failure - they are competing against peers who have a calibrated assistant. Point them at the allowed lane explicitly and pair them with a group member who uses it well.
  • The co-authored paper with another group: your norms travel with your name. Agree the AI-use ground rules with the collaborating PI at project start, the same way you would agree authorship order - the strictest applicable rule wins, as always.
  • Suspected fabrication in submitted work: that is no longer a norms conversation - it is your institution's research-integrity process. Know the referral path before you need it. The fabricated-citation detection deep-dive lives in the AI + Law course.
Demo

Draft your group's authorship-and-AI one-pager, live ★ 18 min · everyone builds

Not a template handout - you write yours, section by section, in the session, so that what leaves the room is already in your voice and matched to your group's actual work. Five sections, one page, dated.

Header: "AI use in [your group] - working norms, drafted [today's date], review quarterly". The date matters: policies move, and a dated document invites revision instead of ossifying.

Section 1 - allowed with conditions: adapt the left lane to your field. Name the actual tasks your group does (screening abstracts? analysis code? manuscript polish?) and the condition attached to each. Specific beats noble.

Section 2 - never: copy the right lane almost verbatim - fabricating anything, uploading unpublished or participant data, delegating conclusions. These three do not vary by field.

Section 3 - disclosure defaults: the ICMJE pattern as your house style. Writing assistance goes in acknowledgments, analysis use in methods, both flagged in the cover letter - and internally, every manuscript carries an AI-use note, even when it reads "none".

Section 4 - the verification habit: one sentence. "If AI assisted, you can show what you checked." Then the referral line: where questions go (you), and where suspected fabrication goes (the integrity office, not a group meeting).

Sign it first. Your name at the bottom, before anyone else's, with your own disclosure practice stated. Then take it to group meeting as a draft for annotation, not an edict for compliance.

★ The thirty-second disclosure template your group reuses forever

During the preparation of this work the authors used [tool] to [specific task: e.g. improve the clarity of author-written text / assist with literature screening]. The authors reviewed and verified all content affected [state how: e.g. all suggested references were resolved to DOIs and read] and take full responsibility for the content of the published article.

Why annotation beats announcement A one-pager you hand down gets filed; a one-pager your group marked up gets followed. Budget one group meeting for the students to challenge the draft - the places they push back are exactly the places the norms were about to be ignored. You will also learn, faster than any detector could tell you, what they are actually using.
Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 45-60 min total

  • Finish the one-pager and put it on the group meeting agenda within two weeks, framed as a draft for annotation. Note what surprises you in the discussion - that is your real usage baseline.
  • Disclose first: add an AI-use note (even "none") to the manuscript you are currently closest to submitting, using the template. Mention in group meeting that you did.
  • Check your own institution's guidance on AI in supervision and student work - it may bind you more tightly than any journal policy, and in a proceeding it is the document that counts. Verify the current version.
  • Read the Liang et al. (2023) Patterns paper abstract before you next hear a detector pitch - ten minutes now saves one bad accusation later.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Why does every major publisher refuse AI authorship - what is the load-bearing rationale?

The rationale is accountability, and it matters because it generates every downstream answer: the AI goes where instruments and assistance go (acknowledgments, methods), never where responsibility sits (the byline). A student who knows the rationale can derive the rules; one who only knows the rule stays confused at the edges.

2 · Roughly 70% of journals have AI policies, yet only ~0.1% of post-2023 papers disclosed AI use. What is the supervision lesson?

A 700-fold gap between policy coverage and disclosure behaviour is not a fraud epidemic - it is rational silence under ambiguity and stigma. The fix within your reach is cultural: a thirty-second template, an AI-use note on every manuscript including "none", and the PI's own disclosure made visibly first.

3 · What separates provenance (like Grammarly Authorship) from detection, and why does it matter for supervision?

Detection is an after-the-fact guess with documented bias (Liang et al., Patterns 2023) - it points suspicion, disproportionately at international students. Provenance is a log kept during writing that an honest author can show voluntarily. One flips the incentive toward hiding; the other toward documenting. Supervision should reward documentation.
