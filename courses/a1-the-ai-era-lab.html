Learn AI Academia with Phoebe · Leader track · Session 1 of 6

The AI-era lab: what changed, what broke, what you decide

You do not get to decide whether AI enters your lab. It arrived through the search engines, the reference managers and your students' drafts before anyone asked you. The question you do own is under which rules it operates - and that question rewards the leader who has seen the honest capability map and read the failure catalogue with the receipts attached. This session gives you both, plus the reason the two easy answers - ban it, embrace it - both fail a lab in practice. No code today; judgement only.

Part 0

Why the leader track starts here

Every lab head is currently being offered two ready-made opinions: the vendor's ("your group is leaving productivity on the table") and the common room's ("this is the end of scholarship"). Both save you the trouble of thinking, and both will cost you later. This track replaces borrowed opinions with three things you can actually lead with: what the tools verifiably do, what has verifiably gone wrong when nobody owned the output, and the explicit norms that sit between a ban and a free-for-all. Session 1 is the evidence; sessions a2-a6 are the rules you will build on it.

Part 1 · what changed

The capability map, at leader altitude 8 min live

You do not need to know how a transformer works to run a lab well in 2026. You need one asymmetry: these tools are strong wherever the task is high-volume text work with a human check at the end, and weak - reliably, measurably weak - wherever the task is being right about the world. Every rule you will write in this track is downstream of that one sentence.

LiveIt is already in the building4 min▶

Three places AI reached your lab before you formed a policy:

  • Search. The tools your group already uses for discovery - Semantic Scholar and friends - ship AI-assisted features as defaults, not options. The researcher track (b2) walks the toolbox; you only need to know it is the water they swim in.
  • Reference managers and writing tools. AI arrives via plugins and built-in assistants, often without anyone consciously adopting it. "We do not use AI here" is, in most labs, already factually false.
  • Your students' drafts. Not necessarily disclosed, not necessarily misused - but present. The interesting leadership question is not whether, it is under which rules, and right now the honest answer in most groups is "none written down".

That last gap is the whole reason this track exists. Drift is a policy too - just one nobody chose and nobody can defend in a misconduct hearing.

Self-studyHow weak is weak? The numbers behind the right column3 min read▶

The right column is not hand-waving. Published audits of fabricated citations found rates of 55% for GPT-3.5 and 18% for GPT-4 (Walters & Wilder 2023), with Chelli et al. (2024) reporting similar ranges across models. Newer models and retrieval grounding cut the rate but never to zero - and a fabricated reference is visually indistinguishable from a real one until someone resolves it. The researcher track makes your students measure their own fabrication tally in b1; the deep-dive detection technique lives with our sibling course AI + Law, and claim-vs-source auditing lives with AI + Research. Your job as leader is simpler: never let "the AI found this reference" pass as a provenance statement in your lab.

Part 2 · what broke

The failure catalogue, with receipts 9 min live

Four cases, all real, all citable, all worth retelling at your next lab meeting. Notice what they have in common before the diagram tells you: in every one, the tool behaved exactly as tools behave. What failed was a human checkpoint - an author who did not read, a reviewer who did not look, an editor who waved it through.

LiveThe two retractions, told properly4 min▶
  • "Certainly, here is a possible introduction for your topic:" - that chatbot preamble sat as the opening line of a materials-science paper in Surfaces and Interfaces (Elsevier), published in 2024 and retracted in May 2024 for undisclosed AI use. Count the humans who had the chance to read one sentence: the authors, every co-author, the handling editor, the reviewers. All of them missed the first line of the introduction.
  • The Frontiers rat figure (February 2024). An AI-generated anatomical figure - impossible in ways any first-year biology student would catch - made it through review and into print. The internet caught it within hours; the journal retracted within days. The speed of the retraction is the tell: the error was never hard to see. Nobody with responsibility looked.
Real world

Both cases are catalogued with primary links at Academ-AI, which tracks suspected undeclared AI in published papers. When you retell these at a lab meeting, the point to land is not "AI is dangerous" - it is "four humans in a row assumed someone else had read it". That failure mode predates AI; AI just industrialised the volume of unread text.

LiveThe scale evidence: this is not a few bad apples3 min▶
  • Tortured phrases. Cabanac et al. (2021) named the pattern - automatic paraphrases that mangle fixed terms into nonsense - and found them in published, peer-reviewed venues. The Problematic Paper Screener grew out of that work and now flags candidates across the literature continuously. The lesson for a leader: detection at the ecosystem level exists, but it works after publication, when the damage is reputational and shared.
  • Liang et al. (ICML 2024). Between 6.5% and 16.9% of peer-review text at major AI conferences was estimated to be LLM-modified. Read that carefully: the checkpoint that is supposed to catch unowned text is itself producing unowned text. The review duty question gets a full session in a5.
Self-studyWhat the catalogue does not prove3 min read▶

Resist the tempting over-read. The catalogue does not show that AI use causes misconduct, and it does not justify treating every disclosed use as a scandal. Plenty of careful, disclosed, policy-compliant AI assistance is happening in the same literature and by definition never makes this list. What the catalogue shows is narrower and more useful: every public failure so far is a failure of human ownership at a checkpoint - unread text, unexamined figures, undisclosed use where disclosure was owed. That is excellent news for a lab head, because ownership checkpoints are exactly the thing a leader can mandate. You cannot make the tools truthful; you can make your people readers of record. Deep-dive techniques live with our sibling courses: fabricated-citation detection with AI + Law, claim-vs-source auditing with AI + Research.

Part 3 · what you decide

Why "ban it" and "embrace it" both fail a lab 6 min live

Faced with the catalogue, most leaders reach for one of two levers. Both are comfortable. Both are wrong, and the evidence for why is worth having at your fingertips before a faculty meeting reaches for either.

LiveThe ban: comfortable, unenforceable, and quietly unjust3 min▶
  • Bans push use underground. The tools are free, invisible in output when used with minimal care, and embedded in software your group already runs. A ban does not stop use; it stops disclosure - which means you lose exactly the visibility a leader needs, while keeping all of the risk.
  • Enforcement rests on detectors, and detectors are unreliable. Worse than unreliable: Liang et al. (Patterns 2023) showed GPT detectors are biased against non-native English writers, flagging their genuine writing at markedly higher rates. Verify the current literature before quoting any exact figure - the bias finding itself is solid. A ban enforced by detection means your international students carry the false-accusation risk for everyone. That is not a policy; it is a liability.
  • Bans also ban the left column. The defensible uses - triage, drafting with review, sparring - are real productivity your competitors' labs are keeping.
LiveThe naive embrace: how you end up in the catalogue3 min▶

"Use whatever makes you productive" sounds modern and trusting. Walk it forward: no disclosure norms, so you cannot answer a journal's disclosure requirement honestly. No verification norms, so fabricated citations ride into manuscripts with your name on them. No confidentiality norms, so a well-meaning student pastes an unpublished collaborator draft into a consumer chat window. Every entry in Part 2's catalogue happened inside institutions that had not written their rules yet. The naive embrace is not a policy either - it is the drift from Part 1, wearing enthusiasm as a costume.

The middle is explicit norms - and it is the rest of this track Not a mood, a document: who may use what, on which artifacts, with what disclosure, verified how, and who signs as reader of record. a3 builds the supervision and authorship rules, a4 the grant rules, a5 the peer-review rules, a6 assembles the one-page lab policy live. By a6 you will have written it, not downloaded it.

Demo 1 of 1

Reading a retraction notice together ★ 12 min · read along

The leader-track version of a build-along: we put the "Certainly, here is" case on the projector and read it the way a misconduct panel would - checkpoint by checkpoint. By the end you can run this same exercise with your own group, which is worth more than any slide deck about AI ethics.

Pull up the case: Zhang et al., Surfaces and Interfaces (Elsevier), retracted May 2024, catalogued with primary links at Academ-AI. Read the paper's first line of the introduction aloud: "Certainly, here is a possible introduction for your topic:". Let the room sit with it.

Checkpoint 1 - the drafting author. What would have caught it: reading your own manuscript once, start to finish, before circulating. Ask the room: does anyone believe this author read the introduction?

Checkpoint 2 - the co-authors. Every name on the byline certified this text. What would have caught it: a norm that co-authorship means having read the whole paper, stated out loud in your lab rather than assumed.

Checkpoint 3 - editor and reviewers. The first sentence of the introduction is arguably the most-read sentence in any manuscript. It still passed. What would have caught it: reviewers reading rather than skimming - which loops back to Liang et al.'s finding about what is happening to review text itself.

Close with the leader question: which of these checkpoints do you control in your lab, starting Monday? Write the answer in one sentence. Most rooms land on the same one: "in this group, nobody submits or signs what they have not read." That sentence is the seed of your a6 policy.

Why read a retraction and not a success story Success stories with AI are private and unverifiable; failures are public and documented. A retraction notice is the rare artifact where the whole safeguard chain is visible in one place - and reading one together makes "human ownership" concrete in a way no policy memo manages.
Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 30-40 min total

  • Run the retraction-reading exercise from the demo at your next lab meeting, using the same case. Budget 15 minutes. End on the one-sentence lesson and write down what your group actually says - you will want it in a6.
  • Take a quiet inventory: where is AI already in your group's workflow? Ask two students informally (not as an audit - as curiosity). The gap between what you assumed and what you hear is your real starting point.
  • Browse the Academ-AI catalogue for ten minutes and pick one case from your own field. Having a discipline-local example doubles the impact of every conversation that follows.
  • Write down, without polishing, your current instinct: ban, embrace, or norms? Keep the note. You will grade it against your own a6 policy in five sessions.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · What do the four catalogue cases - the "Certainly" retraction, the rat figure, tortured phrases, the LLM-modified reviews - actually share?

In every case the tool behaved as tools behave; what failed was a human who was supposed to read, look or own the output and did not. That diagnosis matters because ownership checkpoints are exactly what a lab head can mandate - you cannot make models truthful, but you can make your people readers of record.

2 · Why can't you enforce a lab-wide AI ban by running a detector over drafts?

The bias finding is the decisive one for a leader: detector-based enforcement puts your international students at disproportionate risk of false accusation over their genuine writing. Combined with the fact that bans push use underground and kill disclosure, detection-backed prohibition costs you visibility and fairness at the same time.

3 · If bans fail and naive embrace fails, what does the leader track build instead?

The middle between prohibition and drift is a document, not a mood: who may use what, on which artifacts, with what disclosure, verified how, and who signs as reader of record. That is the arc of a3-a6 - and waiting is just the naive embrace with a clearer conscience, since the tools are already in the building.
