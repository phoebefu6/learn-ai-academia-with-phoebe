The literature you need exists; the question is whether your search can see it. You leave with a search string tested against papers you already know must be found - which is the closest thing to measuring your own recall for free.

Part 1 · the stack

The search stack that is actually free 7 min live

Three jobs, three shelves. A baseline engine for quick coverage checks, structured search with an API for strings you can log and rerun, and graph tools that walk outward from a paper instead of a keyword. None of it costs money to start - and each shelf covers a failure mode of the others.

LiveThree engines, three failure modes4 min▶
ToolWhat it verifiably isWhat it will not do for you
Google ScholarThe broadest familiar baseline - fast coverage checks, citation countsNo API, ranking you cannot inspect, searches you cannot log or rerun exactly
Semantic ScholarFree academic search from the non-profit Ai2, 200M+ papers, free APICoverage varies by field - pair it, never marry it
OpenAlexFully open scholarly index, CC0 data - the plumbing under many other toolsIt is infrastructure, not an interface: strongest through its API and the tools built on it

The working pattern: draft and test your string where you can log it (Semantic Scholar, OpenAlex, your field's own database), then use Google Scholar as the cross-check that nothing obvious is missing. A search you cannot rerun is a search you cannot defend in a methods section.

LiveGraph discovery: three map tools, one idea3 min▶

Keyword search finds papers that share your vocabulary. Graph tools find papers that share your papers - through citations and similarity, which is exactly where the vocabulary problem cannot hide.

  • Connected Papers builds a similarity graph from a single seed paper - one screen showing the neighbourhood, its clusters, its prior and derivative works.
  • ResearchRabbit keeps a free-forever tier and works from collections rather than single seeds; it was acquired by Litmaps in 2025, so watch how the tiers evolve.
  • Litmaps free tier gives you 20 inputs and 2 maps - enough to test whether the mapping workflow earns a place in your routine before any money moves.

All three sit on open citation data of the kind OpenAlex publishes. They are discovery aids, not coverage guarantees: what is not indexed is not on the map, a limit b3 turns into its honest-ceiling lesson.

Self-studyWhy the API matters even if you never write code2 min read▶

A free API means your search is reproducible in principle: the same query, rerun next month, with the differences inspectable. Even if you personally stay in the web interface, choosing tools with open data and APIs means your review can graduate later - to scripted searches, to a PRISMA-grade audit trail, to handing a collaborator the exact query instead of a description of it. Closed ranking is fine for a first look; it is a poor foundation for a claim about what the literature says.

Part 2 · the craft

Boolean craft, synonym harvesting, and the snowball 7 min live

The single biggest silent failure in literature search is vocabulary: the same intervention can carry six names across subfields and decades. A reviewer who searches one term finds one slice and honestly believes it is the field. The craft below is how you beat that without any AI at all - and it is what makes the AI tools safe to add later.

LiveSynonym harvesting: mine the papers, not your imagination4 min▶

Do not invent your synonym list at a blank page. Take the first three genuinely relevant papers you have and mine them: the author keywords, the title phrasing, the terms in the abstract that are not yours. Each subfield's dialect is written down in its own papers - harvesting beats brainstorming every time.

The naive string

retrieval practice university

The harvested string

("retrieval practice" OR "testing effect" OR "practice testing" OR "test-enhanced learning" OR "low-stakes quizzing" OR "successive relearning") AND (undergraduate OR university OR "higher education") AND (retention OR "long-term learning")

Two craft notes. First, group synonyms with OR inside parentheses, then connect the concept groups with AND - one group per concept, never one giant soup. Second, most databases let you scope a term to title and abstract fields rather than full text; the field-code syntax differs per database, so check its help page rather than guessing. Scoping cuts noise dramatically once your synonym groups are wide.

LiveThe known-set spot check: recall measured for free3 min▶

Before you trust any string, write down 5 papers you already know MUST be in scope - from your supervisor, from a course, from your own reading. Run the string. Count how many of the 5 come back.

  • 5 of 5: the string passes its smoke test. It may still miss unknowns, but it is not broken on the knowns.
  • Fewer: open each missed paper and find out why - almost always a vocabulary gap. Add the missing synonym group and rerun until all 5 return.

This is the cheapest measurement in all of research methodology: a recall check that costs ten minutes and requires no ground truth beyond what is already in your head. b3's screening lab turns this exact move into one of its levers.

Self-studySnowballing as a discipline, not a vibe3 min read▶

Everyone snowballs casually - you read a paper, you chase a citation. The disciplined version differs in three ways:

  • Both directions, deliberately. Backward through the reference list finds the roots; forward through citing papers (Semantic Scholar and the graph tools both show these) finds what was built afterwards, often under newer vocabulary your string never contained.
  • In passes, not wanders. Snowball from your included set, add what qualifies, then snowball the additions. Stop when a pass adds nothing new - that saturation point is a reportable fact about your search.
  • Logged. Every chased paper gets a line: found via which seed, which direction, kept or dropped and why. The log is what turns "I followed some citations" into a method a reviewer can audit.
Part 3 · the shiny buttons

Deep-research agents, in their honest place 5 min live

OpenAI's Deep Research, Gemini Deep Research and Claude's Research mode will plan a multi-step search, browse, and hand you a long cited report on your topic. Used honestly, that is genuinely useful. Used as a substitute for Parts 1 and 2, it is how reviews quietly go wrong.

LiveLeads, not evidence - the rule and the reason3 min▶
  • Quotas are real and volatile. These modes are rationed on every plan tier and the allowances change often - check your plan's current numbers rather than remembering old ones.
  • No published audit measures their citation accuracy in academic domains. That absence is itself the finding: nobody can currently tell you how often these reports cite wrongly. The nearest rigorous evidence is Magesh et al. (2025), a preregistered audit of retrieval-grounded legal AI tools, which found 17-33% hallucination even WITH retrieval grounding. Treat every citation in a generated report as a lead until you have resolved it to a DOI and read it.
  • Right job: a fast first map of unfamiliar territory. New field, new method, a literature you have never touched - a deep-research run gives you names, terms and candidate seed papers in minutes. Those terms feed your synonym harvest; those seeds feed your snowball. The report itself feeds nothing downstream.
The one-line rule A deep-research report may hand you the seed papers and the vocabulary. It is never a screen, never an extraction, and never a literature review - those need the audit trail this track builds in b3 and b4.
Self-studySemantic search engines: what we can and cannot tell you2 min read▶

Tools like Consensus and Perplexity offer semantic search over research - ask a question in plain language, get papers and synthesized answers. Honesty requires a flag here: their current corpus composition and pricing specifics are unverified in this course's source map, so this page will not state numbers for them - check the live pages. The usage rule is the same as for the agent modes regardless: whatever surfaces is a lead to resolve and read, never a fact to cite from the summary.

Demo 1 of 2

Build the search string for YOUR question ★ 12 min · everyone builds

Not a toy exercise: this string is the one your actual review will run on. Bring your real research question and your three most relevant papers.

Write your question as concepts: population, intervention or exposure, outcome. One line each. These become your AND groups.

Harvest synonyms: open your three most relevant papers and copy every keyword, title phrase and abstract term that names your concepts differently. Aim for at least three synonyms per concept before you stop.

Assemble the boolean: OR within each concept group, AND between groups. Scope to title and abstract if your database supports field codes - check its help page for the syntax.

Run the known-set spot check: list 5 papers that must be in scope, run the string on Semantic Scholar or your field's database, count the returns.

Fix until 5 of 5: for each miss, open the paper, find the vocabulary gap, widen the right group, rerun. Save the final string AND the date - it goes in your methods section.

★ Try it now - AI as synonym harvester, with the safety rail

Here are the titles, keywords and abstracts of three papers central to my review: [paste]. List every distinct term these papers use for (1) the intervention, (2) the population, (3) the outcome. Only list terms that literally appear in the text I pasted - do not add terms from your own knowledge. I will widen the net myself from other papers.

Why the "only from the text" rail Restricted to text you supplied, the model is doing extraction - a task you can check in seconds. Unrestricted, it will helpfully invent plausible synonyms, and a plausible-but-unused term costs you nothing while a hallucinated-but-adopted concept can skew a whole search. Same tool, different risk, one sentence apart.
Demo 2 of 2

Walk the graph from your own papers ★ 9 min · explore

Keyword search is done; now the map. You will seed a graph tool with papers you already trust and see what the citation network knows that your vocabulary did not.

Seed ResearchRabbit (free-forever tier) or Connected Papers with your 3 favourite papers - the ones any review of your topic must cite.

Read the map before clicking anything: which clusters do you recognise? Which cluster have you never heard of? That unknown cluster is the map earning its keep.

Write down every surfaced paper you did not know that looks in scope - title and one line on why. This list is tomorrow's reading, not today's.

Chase one backward snowball: open the reference list of your best seed and pull one older paper your string never found.

Chase one forward snowball: open the citing papers of the same seed and pull one newer paper - check what vocabulary it uses. If it names your topic differently, that term goes back into Demo 1's string.

Real world

The forward snowball is where fields change their clothes. The b3 lab's corpus is built on a real case: an intervention that appears as retrieval practice, the testing effect, practice testing, test-enhanced learning, low-stakes quizzing and successive relearning - six names, decades apart, different subfields. No single string catches all six on the first try. The citation graph does not care what anyone calls it - which is exactly why the stack has a graph shelf.

Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 35-45 min total

  • Finish the string if Demo 1 ran out of time: harvest from two more papers, rerun the known-set spot check, and store the final string with its date in your project notes.
  • Run one full snowball pass from your included seeds - backward and forward - with a log line per chased paper. Note where the pass stopped adding new papers.
  • If your topic borders unfamiliar territory, run ONE deep-research query on the unfamiliar part. Highlight every citation in the report, resolve each to a DOI, and keep a tally of how many resolve cleanly. That tally is your personal calibration for these tools.
  • Compare your string's results between Semantic Scholar and Google Scholar. Anything major that only one of them finds tells you about your field's indexing - worth one line in your search log.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Why does a careful, well-spelled keyword search still miss whole slices of a literature?

The vocabulary problem is the field's oldest silent failure: one intervention, six names, and each name marks a subfield you either found or did not. The countermeasures are all in this session - harvest synonyms from real papers, OR them into concept groups, and let the citation graph find what no vocabulary guess could.

2 · What does the known-set spot check actually measure?

You can never measure true recall - the relevant papers you missed are, by definition, invisible to you. But five must-find papers give you a smoke test that costs ten minutes: each miss points at a specific vocabulary gap to fix. It is the single highest-value habit in this session, and b3's lab makes it a lever.

3 · What is the honest role of a deep-research agent report in your review?

No published audit measures these modes' citation accuracy in academic domains - and the nearest rigorous evidence, Magesh et al. (2025) on retrieval-grounded legal tools, found 17-33% hallucination even with grounding. A screen has an audit trail; a report has vibes. Take the vocabulary and the seed papers, resolve everything else to DOIs, and build the review with b3-b4's discipline.
