Grants with AI: assistance yes, substitution never

Grant writing is where AI temptation peaks - the deadline is real, the boilerplate is endless, and the aims page is due. It is also where the stakes are highest: NIH has now said in plain language that substantially-AI-developed applications are not your original ideas, and discovery after award has teeth. This session draws the working line between assistance that is tolerated and substitution that can cost you the grant, and gives you the lab-level workflow that keeps you provably on the right side of it.

Part 1 · the line

The NIH originality line, read practically 8 min live

NIH NOT-OD-25-132 (2025) is short, and its logic is the logic of the whole funding system: they are buying your ideas, not your prose. Everything in this session unfolds from that one sentence. Verify the current version of the notice before your next submission - this territory moves.

LiveNOT-OD-25-132 in five bullets a PI can carry5 min▶
  • The core holding: applications substantially developed by AI are not considered the applicant's original ideas. Originality is the thing being funded; outsource it and the application fails at the definition level, not the quality level.
  • The tolerance: AI assistance on limited aspects is tolerated. The notice does not ban the tools - it bans the substitution of your intellectual work.
  • The test: the team must retain intellectual control of the aims, the rationale, the design, the evidence and the claims. If a reviewer asked "whose idea is this?", every load-bearing element must answer: ours.
  • The teeth: discovery after award is not a shrug. Consequences scale from cost disallowance up to research-misconduct referral. The risk does not expire at the award letter - it attaches to the money.
  • The volume rail: the same notice caps applicants at 6 applications per year - NIH's answer to AI-inflated submission volume. If your group's strategy leaned on quantity, it now leans on quality by rule.
LiveNSF, and the reminder from a2 that reframes everything4 min▶

NSF's stance runs on responsibility rather than prohibition: proposers are responsible for the accuracy and authenticity of their submissions, including any AI-assisted content. A hallucinated reference or an AI-invented preliminary claim in your proposal is your false statement to a federal agency - the tool's involvement is not a defence. Check the current PAPPG language for the year you submit.

And the mirror-image rule from a2: NIH NOT-OD-23-149 bans reviewers from using AI on YOUR application - a reviewer who pastes your aims into a chatbot has breached confidentiality. Hold both notices in one frame and the system's logic becomes visible: your unpublished ideas are treated as confidential, valuable and yours on both sides of the table. The reviewer ban protects your ideas from leaking out; the originality line ensures the ideas being protected are actually yours. Same principle, both directions.

Self-studyWhy "intellectual control" is the phrase to teach your group3 min read▶

Rules phrased as tool bans age badly and invite workarounds. "Intellectual control" is a test that survives every new tool, because it asks about ownership, not software: who conceived the aims, who chose the design, who decided the claims? It also gives your group a usable self-check before any AI touches a proposal document: if this output shaped a load-bearing decision, could we defend that decision as ours without mentioning the tool? If the honest answer is no, the line has been crossed regardless of what the tool was called or how little of its text survived editing.

Part 2 · the boundary

Assistance vs substitution, section by section 8 min live

The notice gives the principle; your group needs the practice. Here is the boundary drawn where proposals actually get written - and the university layer that most PIs discover exists only after they needed it.

LiveYour university has already written rules for this - find them4 min▶

Between you and the funder sits a layer many PIs skip: your own research office, whose guidance binds you and may be stricter than the funder's. This is not hypothetical - real, published examples include Stanford Medicine's 10 rules for AI in proposal work, Utah's Office of Sponsored Projects guidance, and UCSF's Office of Sponsored Research guidance. Three different institutions, the same instinct: enable the assistance, fence the substitution, protect the data.

The action is one email or one search: "[your institution] research office generative AI guidance". Read it before your next deadline, not during it - and remember that in any later question about your conduct, the institutional document is the one you will be measured against. If your research office has not written one yet, this session's boundary diagram is a defensible starting draft to offer them.

LiveDisclosure in grant text: no universal standard, one safe pattern3 min▶

Honesty requires saying this plainly: there is no universal disclosure standard for AI use in grant applications - it is funder-dependent, and the course map flags it as such. What does not vary is the pattern that satisfies any reasonable asker:

  • Tool - what was used, by name and version where sensible.
  • Task - what it did, specifically: "improved clarity of PI-authored text in the significance section", not "assisted with writing".
  • Human verification - who checked the output and how. This third element is what converts a confession into a methods statement.

Where a funder asks for disclosure, this pattern answers it; where a funder is silent, this pattern sits in your internal log (Part 3) ready to be produced. Check the current instructions of the specific funding opportunity - verify the current version - and never treat a journal's disclosure rules as covering your proposals: rules attach to artifacts, and a proposal's rule-holder is the funder.

Self-studyThe boilerplate trap: even the safe lane needs eyes3 min read▶

Facilities and resources sections feel like the free square - descriptive, factual, nobody's intellectual property. Two cautions before you fully relax there:

  • NSF's responsibility standard has no boilerplate exemption. If the AI embellishes your core count, your equipment list or your institutional capabilities, the inaccuracy is yours. Assistance on the prose, verification on every fact - the same discipline, just faster to apply.
  • Boilerplate drifts. A described capability that lapsed two years ago, carried forward by a tool that cheerfully rewrites whatever it is given, is exactly the kind of error that survives every deadline-week read. Date-stamp your boilerplate sources and re-verify them annually, not per proposal.
Part 3 · the record

The grant-office workflow: decisions logged, control signed 6 min live

The difference between a lab that used AI responsibly and a lab that can prove it is about twenty minutes of logging per proposal. Post-award questions arrive months or years later, aimed at whoever's name is on the face page - the workflow below is what lets that person answer from a record instead of a memory.

LiveWhy the sign-off is the PI's job and cannot move down3 min▶

The application goes out under your name, and NOT-OD-25-132's consequences land on the applicant, not the postdoc who ran the tool. The sign-off step exists to force one honest moment before submission: the PI reads the log, checks it against the five-element test (aims, rationale, design, evidence, claims), and signs. Three good things follow:

  • It surfaces surprises pre-submission - the moment to discover a section was AI-drafted is at sign-off, not at a program officer's query.
  • It teaches the group where the line is - people log more carefully when they know the log gets read.
  • It creates the artifact that answers questions later - a dated, signed statement of intellectual control, contemporaneous with the submission. In any post-award inquiry, contemporaneous beats reconstructed, every time.
Real world

"Certainly, here is a possible introduction for your topic:" - the sentence that opened a published, later-retracted Elsevier paper in 2024 is the publishing world's version of the failure this workflow prevents: text that shipped because no human owned it at the last gate. A grant application has one last gate by design - the PI's signature. The sign-off step just makes that gate do its job on the AI question too.

Self-studyTwo edge cases: collaborations and how long to keep the log3 min read▶
  • Multi-institution proposals: your collaborator's research office may draw the line differently from yours, and the submitting institution's rules govern the submission. Agree the AI-use ground rules and who keeps the log at project start - the same conversation as authorship order, had at the same time. As everywhere in this course, the strictest applicable rule is the operative default.
  • Retention: keep the AI-use log and signed PI statement as long as you keep the rest of the grant file - post-award questions under NOT-OD-25-132 can arrive at any point in the award's life, and your institution's records-retention schedule for sponsored projects already answers "how long is that". One folder, one rule, no separate system to forget.
Demo

Red-team your own aims page ★ 18 min · everyone builds

Leaders were asked to bring one aims page (or specific-aims-shaped project summary) to this session. This demo uses the sparring pattern from the researcher track - the one AI use that is safe on grants precisely because it adds nothing: no facts, no citations, no text you might keep. Only objections to ideas that are already yours.

Strip first: remove unpublished preliminary data, collaborator names and anything confidential from the excerpt you will paste. If your institution provides an approved enterprise AI instance, use it; a consumer chat window is not a data enclave either way.

Run the sparring prompt below with your stripped aims. Constraints matter: objections only, ranked, no facts, no citations, no rewrites.

Triage the objections on paper: which would a study section actually raise? Mark each one "defused in the current draft", "needs a sentence", or "needs a think".

Fix the "needs a sentence" items yourself, in your own words, in your own document. The AI critiqued; you author. That division is the whole session in miniature.

Log the exercise: date, tool, "red-teamed aims page, objections only, no generated text retained", verified by you. Your first entry in the Part 3 log - the workflow starts today.

The prompt that crosses the line

Write a specific aims page for an R01 on [topic]. Include three aims, a rationale, and supporting preliminary evidence.

★ The sparring pattern, tuned for aims pages

I am a PI preparing a grant application. Here is my draft specific aims page: [paste stripped version]. Act as a skeptical study-section reviewer in my field. List the six strongest specific objections, ranked by how likely each is to sink the application - feasibility, rigor, significance, innovation, scope. For each: the objection in one sentence, and what a stronger version of the page would show. Do not supply facts, statistics or citations. Do not rewrite any part of the page. Do not compliment the work.

Why this pattern survives every policy Check it against the five-element test: the aims stay yours, the rationale stays yours, the design stays yours, no evidence enters from the model, and every claim in the final document was authored by your team. The model contributed pressure, not substance - which is why this is assistance under NOT-OD-25-132 and its cousins, and why "no facts, no citations, no rewrites" is not a nicety but the load-bearing constraint.
Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 45-60 min total

  • Find your institution's research-office AI guidance and read it against this session's boundary diagram. Note any place it is stricter than the funder - that stricter line is your line.
  • Read the current text of NIH NOT-OD-25-132 (and NOT-OD-23-149) or your primary funder's equivalent - fifteen minutes, primary source, before your next proposal cycle. Verify the current version.
  • Set up the AI-use log for your next proposal: one shared document, the tool-task-verification pattern, one line per decision. Add the PI sign-off text from Part 3 at the bottom, ready to sign.
  • Run the red-team exercise once more on a different section - significance or approach - and time it. Most PIs find the full cycle takes under 30 minutes and reshapes their internal review step permanently.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · What does NIH NOT-OD-25-132 actually penalize?

The notice draws an originality line, not a tool ban: assistance on limited aspects is tolerated, substitution of your intellectual work is not. And the risk survives the award - discovery later can cost the money and escalate to a misconduct referral. Option B describes a rule the notice does not contain; disclosure standards in grant text remain funder-dependent.

2 · Which of these grant uses crosses the assist/substitute line?

The five-element test decides it: aims, rationale, design, evidence, claims stay under the team's intellectual control. Generating the aims outsources the very thing being funded. Polishing PI-authored prose and collecting objections (no facts, no citations, no rewrites) leave every load-bearing element yours - which is what makes them assistance.

3 · Why should an applicant care that reviewers are banned from using AI (NOT-OD-23-149)?

The reviewer ban is confidentiality protection for YOUR ideas: your unpublished aims must not leak through someone else's prompt. Held alongside NOT-OD-25-132, the system's logic is symmetric - your ideas are protected as confidential on the review side and required to be genuinely yours on the application side.
