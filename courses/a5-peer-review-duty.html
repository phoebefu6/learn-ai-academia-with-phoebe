Peer review duty: the strictest room in the building

You review for journals, sit on editorial boards, score grant panels - and every one of those roles now comes with the tightest AI rules in the entire research landscape. This session lays out why: the workload numbers that create the temptation, the detection numbers that prove people are giving in to it, and the red lines that every rule-holder - NIH, Elsevier, Springer Nature, ICMJE, COPE - has drawn in almost identical ink. Then we do the work the honest way, live.

Part 0

Why review gets its own session

Every other stage of research has a negotiation in it - how much AI, disclosed how, allowed where. Review does not. It is the one stage where the funder, the publishers and the ethics bodies all converge on the same short word, and the reason is structural: everything you review is someone else's unpublished work, handed to you in trust. This session is the leader-side treatment; your PhD students get the hands-on version in researcher session b9.

Part 1 · the collision

The workload that tempts, the detection that answers 7 min live

Peer review runs on unpaid, uncredited hours squeezed between everything else, and the volume is staggering. That pressure is real - and so is the evidence that some reviewers have started quietly outsourcing the job. Hold both facts at once, because the rules in Part 2 only make sense against this backdrop.

LiveThe workload numbers, and why they matter to a leader3 min▶

Aczel et al. (2021) estimated that reviewers worldwide spent over 100 million hours on peer review in 2020 - more than 15,000 full-time working years, donated. The Publons Global State of Peer Review (2018) put the annual figure around 68.5 million hours, with an average of 19.1 days per review. These are not trivia: they are the honest explanation of why a tired reviewer with three overdue reports reaches for a shortcut.

As a leader you sit on both sides of this. You feel the load yourself, and you set the norms for the early-career researchers who feel it worse - the ones Liang et al. found most represented in the low-confidence, close-to-deadline reviews. The fix is never "review faster with AI". It is declining invitations you cannot serve honestly, and teaching your group to do the same.

LiveWhat Liang et al. (ICML 2024) actually measured3 min▶

The study estimated the fraction of review text at four AI conferences - ICLR, NeurIPS, CoRL, EMNLP - that was likely substantially modified by an LLM: between 6.5% and 16.9%, depending on venue. Two correlations are the leadership signal:

  • Deadline pressure: the estimated rate rose in reviews submitted close to the deadline.
  • Low confidence: it rose in reviews where the reviewer reported low confidence - exactly the reviews that most needed a careful human.

Note the careful phrasing: the method estimates LLM-modified text at the corpus level; it does not convict any individual review. That distinction matters when you sit in the editor's chair - Part 3 covers what to do with a suspicion.

Self-studyWhere the researcher track picks this up2 min read▶

Part 2 · the red lines

What each rule-holder says, verbatim in spirit 8 min live

Four rule-holders govern your reviewing life, and on this stage they barely disagree. Read the lanes below, then the per-holder detail - and verify the current version of each policy before you next rely on it, because these pages move faster than course pages.

LiveNIH NOT-OD-23-149: the sharpest line anywhere3 min▶

The 2023 NIH notice prohibits the use of generative AI in NIH peer review, full stop. The reasoning it gives is the one to internalise: uploading any part of a grant application to an AI tool discloses confidential material to a third party - a breach of the confidentiality every panel member signs up to. It is not a quality argument ("AI reviews badly"); it is a custody argument ("this material was never yours to share").

Three practical consequences for anyone on a study section or panel:

  • No pasting aims, abstracts, biosketches or any application text into any AI tool, however anonymised you believe it to be.
  • No asking a model to "summarise this proposal" or "draft critique bullets from this section" - both require uploading the material.
  • The prohibition covers the review process, not just the final critique - notes, triage, scoring rationale included.

Notice what the wall is made of. None of these bodies argues that models write useless critiques. Every one of them argues custody: a manuscript under review is unpublished work that the authors handed over in trust, and a reviewer has no right to hand it onward - to a colleague, to a student, or to a model's servers. Self-studyThe held-in-trust principle, stated once and properly2 min read▶

Everything in this session compresses to one sentence: the manuscript is someone else's unpublished work, held in trust, and trust does not have an API. The same principle explains the whole pattern - why the rules bind hardest here, why "but the model helped me review faster" is not a defence, and why the legitimate uses in Part 3 all share one property: they never let the trusted material leave your hands. If your students remember nothing else from your norms conversation, make it this sentence.

Part 3 · the open lane

What a reviewer and an editor may still do 5 min live

The strictest room in the building is not a sealed one. Two uses remain legitimately open to a reviewer - both defined by never touching the manuscript's content - and the editor's chair has its own toolkit for the day a suspicious review lands on the desk.

LiveOpen lane 1: AI on your OWN review text2 min▶

Once you have read the paper and drafted your critique in your own words, polishing the clarity of that draft is your text, not theirs - provided two conditions hold: the journal's rules allow it (ICMJE journals expect you to have asked permission first), and you disclose the use in the review. The manuscript's content still never enters the prompt: you polish sentences like "the statistical treatment of the second experiment is underpowered", not passages quoted from the paper.

★ The shape of a legal polish prompt

Below is my drafted peer-review text, written by me. Improve clarity and tone only: keep every technical judgement exactly as stated, do not add points, do not soften criticisms, do not invent references. My draft: [your own sentences only - nothing quoted or paraphrased from the manuscript].

LiveOpen lane 2: general knowledge, zero manuscript content2 min▶

Asking a model "what are the standard threats to validity in a stepped-wedge trial design?" leaks nothing - it is a textbook question. Asking "does THIS stepped-wedge design have validity threats?" while pasting the methods section leaks everything. The test is mechanical: could this exact prompt have been written by someone who never saw the manuscript? If yes, you are in the open lane.

The line, crossed in one paste

Here is the methods section of a paper I am reviewing: [pasted manuscript text]. What weaknesses should I raise? - This uploads confidential material to a third party. Under NIH rules it is a named confidentiality breach; under Elsevier and Springer Nature rules it is exactly the prohibited act.

Self-studyThe editor's chair: when a review smells generated3 min read▶

COPE publishes case guidance for editors handling suspected AI-generated reviews. The working posture it supports:

  • Suspicion is a process trigger, not a verdict. Generic praise, absent specifics, confident errors about the paper's actual content - these justify a conversation with the reviewer, not an accusation. Detector scores alone convict nobody (the leader track covered detector limits in a3).
  • The remedy is editorial: query the reviewer, weigh the review accordingly, commission a replacement report if confidence is gone, and document the decision.
  • Prevention beats detection: reviewer invitations and journal instructions that state the AI rules explicitly - most breaches in this territory are ignorance, not malice, and a one-line reminder at invitation time is the cheapest fix in publishing.
Demo

Rewrite the sloppy review - no AI near the manuscript ★ 18 min · everyone works

A fictional review of a fictional paper - written for this exercise, resembling far too many real ones. We rebuild it into a review an editor can act on, entirely by hand, then audit which steps could have used AI without breaking a single rule.

The sloppy review - fictional, for this exercise

This paper studies an interesting and timely topic. The writing is generally clear and the methodology seems appropriate. However, the contribution feels somewhat incremental and the related work could be more comprehensive. The experiments are reasonable but more datasets would strengthen the claims. I lean toward rejection but could be convinced otherwise. Minor: some typos.

Diagnose it together: not one sentence refers to anything specific in the paper. Every clause could attach to any manuscript in any field - which is exactly the signature editors now associate with generated reviews, and why vague reviewing has become more expensive than it used to be.

Rebuild the summary: two sentences stating what the paper claims and how it supports the claim - specific enough that the authors would recognise their own work. This is the part only someone who read the paper can write.

Rebuild the criticisms: each vague clause becomes a specific, evidenced point. "Incremental" becomes which prior result it overlaps and where the delta is too small. "More datasets" becomes which population the current data cannot speak for, and why that matters for the central claim.

Separate the verdicts: what must change for acceptance, what would merely be nice, and what is a matter of taste - labelled as such. An actionable review is a sorted review.

Now the audit: which steps could AI have joined legally? Steps 2-4: none - they all require the manuscript. The final clarity pass on YOUR drafted text: yes, with the journal's permission and a disclosure line (Part 3, lane 1). A background question like "what is the standard baseline for this task family?" asked without manuscript content: yes (lane 2). Everything else stays human.

The uncomfortable symmetry to name in the room The sloppy review needed no AI to be worthless - a human wrote its like long before 2023. The rules do not protect us from bad reviews; they protect authors' unpublished work from disclosure. Rigour and confidentiality are two separate duties, and this demo trains both at once.
Homework

Before your next review lands ◐ 25-35 min total

  • Look up the reviewer AI policy of the two journals you review for most, and the current text of NIH NOT-OD-23-149 if you sit on panels. Record the URLs and the date checked - and verify the current version each round, because this territory moves.
  • Write your personal reviewer rule in one sentence and put it where you draft reviews. A workable default: "Nothing from the manuscript enters any tool; AI touches only my own drafted words, with permission and disclosure."
  • Audit your last submitted review against the demo's standard: does every criticism name something specific in the paper? If not, that is your rigour homework - no AI required or allowed.
  • If you edit or chair: add one line about AI rules to your reviewer invitation template, and bookmark COPE's case guidance for the day a generated-looking review arrives.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · What exactly does NIH NOT-OD-23-149 prohibit?

The notice governs peer review, not applicants (applicant-side rules live in NOT-OD-25-132, covered in a4). And it draws no excerpt-sized exception: uploading application material - any of it - discloses confidential material to a third party. The logic is custody, not quality: the application was never the reviewer's to share.

2 · Under ICMJE recommendations, what is the correct order of operations for a reviewer considering any AI use?

ICMJE puts the decision with the journal before any use happens - permission-first - then layers confidentiality and disclosure on top. Option A reverses the order and option B invents a distinction the recommendations do not make. The pattern to teach your group: ask, protect, disclose.

3 · Why is review the strictest stage in the whole AI-and-research landscape?

Every rule-holder - NIH, Elsevier, Springer Nature, ICMJE - grounds its rule in confidentiality and custody, not in output quality. That is why the open lanes in Part 3 all share one property: they never let the trusted material leave your hands. The manuscript is held in trust, and trust does not have an API.
