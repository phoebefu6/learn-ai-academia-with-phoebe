Part 0

Why this course exists

Between the vendor pitch ("AI will do your literature review") and the common-room panic ("AI will end scholarship") there is a narrow, useful strip of ground: what these tools verifiably do, what your institutions verifiably allow, and how to use the first without violating the second. This track walks that strip for one full research cycle - search, screen, extract, hypothesise, code, analyse, write, review - with the receipts attached.

Self-studyDeep-research modes: what the agent buttons actually do4 min read▶

OpenAI's Deep Research, Google's Gemini Deep Research and Claude's Research mode all do a version of the same thing: plan a multi-step search, browse, and return a long cited report. Three honest observations before you lean on them:

  • Quotas are real and volatile. These modes are rationed on every plan tier and the allowances change frequently - check your plan's current numbers rather than remembering old ones.
  • The citations are leads, not evidence. No published audit yet measures citation accuracy of these modes in academic domains. The nearest rigorous study - Stanford's preregistered audit of retrieval-grounded legal AI tools (Magesh et al., 2025) - found 17-33% hallucination even WITH retrieval grounding. Treat every citation in a generated report as unverified until you have resolved it to a DOI and read it.
  • They are a b2 tool, not a b3-b4 replacement. A deep-research report is a fast first map of unfamiliar territory. It is not a screen, not an extraction, and not a literature review - those need the audit trail this track builds.
Part 2 · the rule-holders

The decisions already made for you 7 min live

Before you form an opinion about AI in research, three institutions formed theirs, and they bind you now: your funder, your target journals, and your university. The leader track (a2) reads the policies line by line; here is the researcher's working summary - verify the current version of each before you rely on it, because these are moving.

LiveFunders: the NIH pair everyone should know, whatever your field5 min▶
  • NIH NOT-OD-23-149 (2023): generative AI is prohibited in NIH peer review - a reviewer who pastes any part of your application into an AI tool has breached confidentiality. This is about protecting YOUR unpublished ideas.
  • NIH NOT-OD-25-132 (2025): applications "substantially developed by AI" will not be treated as the applicant's original ideas. AI may assist limited aspects; the aims, rationale, design and claims must remain the team's own intellectual work - and post-award discovery of substantial AI development can escalate to research-misconduct territory.
  • NSF: proposers are responsible for the accuracy and authenticity of their submissions including AI-assisted content, and reviewers face their own restrictions. Check the current PAPPG language for the year you submit.

The shared logic: assistance is tolerated, substitution is not, and your unpublished work must never leak through someone else's prompt. Every funder rule this course cites gets re-verified against the live notice before you rely on it - policy pages change faster than course pages.

LiveJournals: the three-line summary of a messy landscape4 min▶
  • Nobody grants AI authorship. Every major publisher - Springer Nature, Elsevier, Science/AAAS, Wiley, IEEE, ACM - agrees an AI cannot be an author, because authorship carries accountability and a model cannot be accountable.
  • The disclosure line sits at generation, not polish. Springer Nature says AI-assisted copy editing of your own text needs no declaration; Elsevier exempts basic grammar and spelling checks but requires a disclosure statement for generative use in writing. Science has historically been the strictest of the set. The safe default: if AI produced sentences you kept, disclose; if it fixed your grammar, you are in polish territory.
  • Reviewers are locked down hardest. Elsevier and Springer Nature both forbid reviewers from uploading manuscripts to AI tools. ICMJE requires reviewers to ask the journal's permission first and disclose any AI use in the review. Details in b9.
Real world

"Certainly, here is a possible introduction for your topic:" - that sentence opened the introduction of a materials-science paper published by Elsevier in 2024, retracted within months for undisclosed AI use. Nobody involved - authors, editors, reviewers - read the first line carefully enough. The failure was never the tool; it was that no human owned the text. This course exists so your name never sits above a sentence like that.

Self-studyYour university and your data: the two local checks3 min read▶
  • Your university almost certainly has GenAI research guidance already - research offices from Stanford Medicine to Utah to UCSF published theirs within months of the NIH notices. Find yours before assuming; it may be stricter than the funder's, and in a misconduct proceeding it is the document that counts.
  • Unpublished data, participant data, and collaborators' drafts do not go into consumer AI tools. A free-tier chat window is not a data enclave: check the tool's data-retention terms and your ethics approval before anything sensitive crosses that boundary. Human-subjects data has its own rules that no productivity gain overrides. If your institution runs an approved enterprise instance with no-training guarantees, that changes the calculus - which is an a6 (leader track) decision.
Part 3 · the contract

Three ground rules for the whole track 4 min live

Every session that follows assumes these. They are not aspirations; they are the difference between assistance and misconduct.

Demo 1 of 2

The policy check, on your real situation ★ 12 min · everyone builds

Not a toy exercise: by the end of this you know the actual rules that bind YOUR next paper and YOUR next proposal. Bring your laptop; leave with the table filled in.

Open a blank note titled "AI rules that bind me - checked [today's date]". Rules move; the date is part of the record.

Row 1 - university: search "[your university] generative AI research guidance". Record the URL and the one sentence that surprises you most.

Row 2 - funder: find your main funder's current AI notice (NIH: NOT-OD-23-149 and NOT-OD-25-132; NSF: current PAPPG; ERC/others: their latest statement). Record what is prohibited vs disclosed.

Rows 3-4 - your top two target journals: find the publisher's generative-AI policy page. Record: authorship (always no), what needs disclosure and where the statement goes, and the reviewer rule.

Bottom line: write the strictest rule that applies to you in one sentence. That sentence is your personal default from now on.

If two policies conflict They will not conflict by contradiction - they conflict by strictness. A journal that allows disclosed drafting does not override a funder that treats substantial AI development as unoriginal. The strictest applicable rule is always the operative one, and "applicable" follows the artifact: proposals follow funder rules, manuscripts follow journal rules, everything follows your university's.
Demo 2 of 2

First contact, calibrated ★ 10 min · explore

Two prompts on a topic you know deeply - your own thesis area. The point is to feel both edges of the tool in the domain where you cannot be fooled.

Ask a general assistant: "Give me 8 key papers on [your exact thesis topic] with authors, venues and years." Then check every one against Semantic Scholar. Count the fabrications and the subtle manglings - wrong venue, wrong year, merged authors.

Now ask: "Here is my one-paragraph study design. List the five strongest objections a hostile reviewer would raise." Notice the difference in quality - no facts were requested, so nothing could be fabricated; the reasoning is where the value is.

Write down your calibration sentence: what this tool is for (structured sparring over content you supply) and not for (facts, references, anything you cannot personally verify).

★ Try it now - the sparring pattern you will reuse all course

I am a researcher in [field]. Here is my draft [research question / design / abstract]: [paste yours]. Act as a skeptical reviewer at a top venue in my field. List the five strongest specific objections, ordered by how likely they are to sink the paper. For each: the objection in one sentence, and what evidence would defuse it. Do not compliment the work. Do not invent citations.

Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 30-40 min total

  • Finish the policy-check table if any row is blank, and put a quarterly reminder in your calendar to re-verify it - every policy in it has changed at least once since 2023.
  • Install the free stack: a Semantic Scholar account, Zotero, and one discovery tool (ResearchRabbit or Connected Papers). Seed the discovery tool with your three favourite papers and note what it surfaces that you did not know.
  • Run the fabrication test from Demo 2 on a second topic you know well, and keep the tally. Your personal fabrication rate estimate is worth more than any benchmark.
  • Find out whether your institution provides an enterprise AI instance with data protections - one email to your research office. The answer changes what you can do in b4 and b7.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Where is AI assistance strongest in the research lifecycle, and where is it weakest?

Ranking 3,000 abstracts, drafting boilerplate code and tightening prose are volume problems with a verification step. Whether a citation is real, an analysis sound, or a claim true - the things careers stand on - are exactly where the tools are least reliable. The map is uneven, and the gold box (claims and conclusions) never delegates.

2 · Your funder allows disclosed AI assistance; your target journal exempts only grammar checks. Which rule do you work to?

Policies attach to artifacts. Your proposal is governed by the funder's notice, your manuscript by the journal's policy, and everything you do by your university's guidance. Within one artifact, when rules overlap, the strictest applicable one is the operative default - a one-line disclosure costs nothing.

3 · Why does ground rule 1 say every AI-suggested reference must resolve to a DOI and be read?

Walters & Wilder (2023) measured 55% fabrication for GPT-3.5 and 18% for GPT-4; Chelli et al. (2024) found similar ranges. Newer models and retrieval grounding cut the rate but never to zero. A fabricated reference is indistinguishable from a real one until you resolve it - which is why resolving it is the rule.
